Workspaces & Invites

Signing in

Settings → Account. Sign in with GitHub, the one provider the sign-in screens offer.

Slate opens your system browser, you approve there, and the provider comes back to a loopback address that Slate's own local server is listening on. The server does the code exchange itself, so no code, token, or password ever passes through the app's UI. The resulting session is written to a 0600 file (cloud-session.json) in Slate's app-data folder rather than inside any database — exporting a workspace must never hand someone a live refresh token — and the local server owns it because Slate's MCP sidecar needs the same session for shared recall.

Your first sign-in provisions a personal account. Everyone signs in on first launch (ADR 0103); after that, Personal works offline and never syncs until you share it.

Settings → Account shows who you are signed in as, and is where Sign out lives. Below your name it shows your Plan as two cards.

  • Free is everything Slate does on this Mac. It is your plan unless you are in early access.
  • Team is sharing a workspace with your team. It is coming soon and has no price yet. Tell me when it's ready records that you want to hear, and the button then reads We'll email you. If you are in Team early access, the card says Early access instead.

Your work stays on this Mac on every plan. The account holds only who you are and your plan.

Sharing is Team. On Free, a new workspace is on this machine only, and the sharing switch is off and disabled with a Team · coming soon note and the same button. A workspace that is already shared can still be turned off. With Team early access, sharing works as before.

Two tiers, because one isn't enough

TierWhat it means
AccountThe billing/organisation tier. A company, or one person. Roles: owner, admin, member.
WorkspaceOne store with one audience, inside an account. Roles: owner, editor, viewer.

Membership in a workspace is independent of membership in the account that owns it. That is the point: a contractor gets one workspace and not the company; a founder who administers the account can reach a workspace inside it without being separately added, so nobody can be locked out of their own company.

Settings → Workspaces lists every workspace you belong to, across all accounts — not the workspaces of one account. An account-scoped list would show you your own empty personal account and hide the shared workspace you were actually invited to.

What a role controls

A role gates two things: administration, and whether you can write at all.

RoleCan readCan write pagesCan invite, remove and renameCan grant the owner role, or delete the workspace forever
owneryesyesyesyes
editoryesyesnono
vieweryesnonono

The account that holds a workspace adds two more kinds of administrator. The account's owner can do everything a workspace owner can. The account's admins can invite, remove and rename, but not more. Only a workspace owner or the account's owner can make someone an owner, change or remove an owner, or delete the workspace forever. An account admin cannot make themselves an owner. The database enforces this, not the app.

What a role does not gate is whose pages you may edit. That is already author-only: nobody can change anyone else's page whatever their role, which is what keeps merge conflicts out of the design entirely. An editor's right is the right to publish their own work.

A viewer's replica is read-only on your machine too, not just on the server. Postgres would refuse a viewer's rows on the way up, and a refusal that only happens on the way up is the worst kind: you write pages, believe the workspace has them, and nobody ever sees them. So the role is recorded locally and the write is refused where it is made, with a line saying an editor or the owner can change it. A viewer's machine does not push at all.

Accepting an invitation fetches your role straight away.

The same refusal applies to a memory an AI tool or you try to save there. Until the role has been fetched — offline, signed out, or on a machine that joined before this existed — the workspace is treated as writable. Refusing writes on the strength of something never fetched would break the offline promise, and the cloud still has the last word.

Inviting someone

Settings → Workspaces, click the workspace's row, open Members, then type an email address and press Create invitation. The invitation is for the editor role. Slate does not send an email. The form says so up front: creating an invitation saves it, and you send it yourself from the pending row (see Telling them about it).

An invitation is a membership addressed by an email instead of a user id. It has to work this way: workspace membership keys on a user id, and a user id only exists once that person has signed in at least once. Without an invitation row, a workspace would be reachable only by people who were somehow already in it.

The address is stored lower-cased and trimmed — the database enforces it — so an invite cannot sit unclaimable next to a differently-cased login.

Accepting is explicit. It used to happen silently on sign-in, which meant an invitation always "arrived" even when nobody had told the person anything, and there was no way to decline. Now a pending invitation appears as a banner at the top of the app, whichever screen you are on, naming the workspace, who invited you, and the role, with Accept and Decline. Accepting provisions the replica and the workspace appears in the switcher. A declined invitation stays declined: nothing joins you to that workspace unless you are invited again.

The banner is in the shell rather than on a screen on purpose. Administration lives in Settings, which nobody opens speculatively — so a banner living there would be an invitation nobody is ever shown, which is the exact failure explicit acceptance was introduced to fix.

Pending invitations are listed alongside members, and can be revoked before they are claimed.

Telling them about it

Slate has no mail server of its own — there is no server in this design at all — so it writes the invitation and hands it to something that can already deliver. Every pending row carries two actions:

ActionWhat happens
EmailYour default mail app opens with the invitation composed — addressed, subject filled, body written. You read it and press send.
CopyThe same message goes to your clipboard, for Slack, Teams, or wherever your team actually talks.

The message names the workspace, says who invited them, links to where Slate is installed from, and — the line that matters most — states the exact address the invitation is tied to. Membership is claimed by email, so someone who signs in with their other account finds nothing and has no way to know why.

The mail arrives from you, not from a no-reply address. That is the upside of not having a mail server: better deliverability, and a recipient who recognises the sender. The cost is one click — Slate composes it, you send it.

Creating and deleting a workspace forever

New workspace in Settings → Workspaces asks for a name, a colour and whether to share it, and Create workspace makes it. Sharing starts on and needs you signed in. Switched off, the workspace is a file on this machine only, and you can share it later. A shared one belongs to your account, you are its first member, and its replica is provisioned as part of creating it — a workspace you can see in the switcher and cannot write to would be worse than one that was never created.

Sync now sits beside it. Sharing happens every couple of minutes anyway; the button is for when you do not want to wait, and it reports what moved. What an action did is shown as a status note under the buttons, in its own colour, so a success never looks like an error.

Each workspace gets a colour so two are told apart at a glance. It is not only the switcher's dot — the Sessions tab paints every place, limb and node in the colour of the workspace it runs in, so what is yours and what is work read apart on the one screen that shows both. There are nine to pick from: the five of Slate's mark (blue, teal, green, amber, red), which new workspaces get in that order, and four more (cyan, purple, pink, lime) so a sixth workspace need not repeat one. Under the monochrome themes, Onyx and Graphite, each of the four takes the grey of its nearest mark colour. The colour is yours alone: stored on your machine, not on the shared row, so everyone keeps their own and anyone can recolour regardless of their role.

Delete workspace forever is in the same workspace dialog, under General, and it is the one genuinely destructive action in the cloud layer: it removes every page everyone put into that workspace, for every member, with no notice to them. Authors keep whatever they moved back out; the shared rows are gone. Only a workspace owner or the account's owner can do it, and Slate asks twice. For anyone else the button changes nothing.

Your own replica is marked read-only rather than removed. Content you can still see is content you can still copy out, and a store vanishing because a server said so is not something a local-first app should do — removing it is then your decision.

Removing someone

Removing a workspace member revokes their read access on the next request they make; Row Level Security decides what they can see on every query, so there is no cached grant to expire.

On their machine, the workspace stops appearing in the cloud's list, and the next launch marks their replica read-only. They keep reading and exporting what is in it — it was on their disk — but nothing more arrives and nothing more goes up.

Pages they authored stay in the workspace, since those rows belong to them as author. If they want them gone, that is Erase my work, which tombstones every row they authored so every member's replica drops its copy on the next pull.
