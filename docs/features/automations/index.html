Features

Automations

Slate runs seven things on its own. Each is invisible while it works, which is fine until you want to know what the app is doing right now, or make it stop.

Connect → Automations answers both. Every automation says what it does and what turning it off costs, and every one of them has a switch. It sits in Connect, beside what this workspace runs and what it can reach.

Whose background it runs in

The page is in two groups, and the heading a switch sits under answers the question people actually have: does turning this off affect my other workspace?

This workspace. Six of them act on one workspace's own rows — its routines, its pages, its sessions, its Work items. Turning one off in Personal leaves Work running.

This Mac. The other three govern something there is only one of on this computer: the capture that reads your transcripts, the app that updates itself, and whether the computer may sleep. Turning one off turns it off everywhere.

A switch takes effect on the next pass, not the next launch. Each loop asks whether it is allowed to run every time it ticks, so turning something off stops it before its next tick — within a minute for the routine tick, within two for sync.

What none of them do

Two of these reach the network on Slate's own account, and both are named in Privacy & Security: the update check asks for a release manifest, and shared-workspace sync mirrors the workspaces you joined. A third — scheduled routines — reaches it only through the brain CLI it starts, under the auth you already gave that tool. The rest are entirely local: they read files on your disk and write rows to your own database, keep-awake only asks the operating system not to sleep, and the session notification is shown by macOS on this Mac alone.

Turning something off never deletes anything. Capture off does not remove sessions already captured; sync off does not withdraw pages already shared (that is leaving a workspace); startup repairs off does not undo repairs already made.

Keeping the computer awake

A routine that starts at 7:00 does not run if the computer went to sleep at 6:45, and a long agent session stops when the lid of an idle machine is left open long enough. This switch stops that — only while there is work.

It counts as work: a session in Sessions whose status is working, a routine that is running, and a turn of Slate's own agent. A session that is only waiting for you does not count. When the last piece of work ends, the computer may sleep again on its usual schedule.

You can turn it on in two places, and both change the same switch:

  • the coffee-cup button at the right end of the top bar, on every screen. It turns green, with a live dot, while it is holding the computer awake. Click it to see what is holding it;
  • the Keep the computer awake row here, under This Mac.

What it does not do:

  • It never keeps the screen on. The display turns off on its usual schedule.
  • It never stops you putting the computer to sleep from the menu. On a Mac on battery, closing the lid still sleeps it.
  • It never outlives Slate. The request ends when Slate quits, or even crashes, so the computer is never left stuck awake.

On macOS this is the same request caffeinate -i makes, and pmset -g assertions lists it under Slate.

Startup repairs, honestly

This is the one switch that can leave you worse off. The repairs exist because earlier versions of Slate wrote data that later versions know how to fix — a page indexed with empty search text, a routine pointing at a page in the wrong store. They are re-derivations rather than guesses, and each runs once rather than on every boot.

Turn them off and Slate stops correcting itself. Anything a past version broke stays broken, and a repair shipped in a future release will not reach your data until this is switched back on. The switch is here because a thing that rewrites your database on launch should be something you can see and stop, not because turning it off is a good idea.
