Features

Work

Work is the place for what you owe: an issue assigned to you, a pull request someone asked you to review, a message waiting for your reply — and the lines you write yourself, which no tool reports. Any of them can carry a time, and Slate rings when it comes. Work sits in the rail right after Sessions.

Today's meetings

When a work toolkit you connected carries a Google Calendar, one Today row sits above the list and the Map:

  • The next meeting on the left: its time, "in 25 min" within the hour and the clock after that ("now" while one runs), its name, and Join when it has a video link.
  • The day as a thin line, 08 to 19, widened when a meeting falls outside it. Each meeting is a mark; a dashed mark is one you have not answered or said maybe to. A red tick marks now, and the hours are labelled at 09, 12, 15 and 18.
  • How the day adds up on the right: how many meetings, how many all-day events, and how much free time is left — only gaps of 45 minutes or more count.

Click any meeting — its mark, or the next meeting's name — to open its card: the title, the times and length, how far off it is ("in 25 min", "now · ends in 12 min", "ended"), your answer, and Join and Open in Calendar. When the title names one of your Work items by its key ("Sync on PROJ-123"), or a pull request by its number ("Review #412", when only one of your items has that number), the card lists it under About, and clicking it opens that item — with what Slate remembers about it and Start — to prepare. It closes with its ✕, Escape, or a click anywhere else.

Meetings you declined, and cancelled ones, are left out. A meeting is not something you owe, so it is not in a section, has no Done and adds nothing to any count.

Slate reads the calendar itself with every Work sync, through the same toolkit that reads your Jira, Slack and Gmail. It costs no tokens and works whichever AI tool the project uses. If the calendar needs you to sign in again, the strip says so, with a Sign in button when the toolkit gave a link. After you press it the strip checks again every few seconds, so the meetings appear on their own once the browser sign-in finishes; after three minutes with no answer it says so. A workspace with no calendar in its toolkits shows no strip.

Today, Work reads GitHub, and Jira, Slack and Gmail through a company tool gateway set up in your AI tool. Find my work tools can teach it any other tool your AI tool reaches (see below). A tool Slate recognises but cannot read yet says Soon.

Tasks

New task, at the top of the Work pane — where Projects has New project and Loop has New routine — writes something nothing else reported: "call the accountant", "renew the domain". Type it, press Enter. The box stays open, so three things go in as three lines. Set a reminder first if it needs one.

They go in Tasks, a section of their own. Not into Solve, Review or Reply: those three are a map of other people's inboxes, and whether calling the accountant is a Solve or a Reply is a question with no useful answer. Nothing you write lands in them, and no tool can put anything in Tasks.

The list is grouped by when: Overdue, Today, Tomorrow, This week, Later, then the ones with no time on them. Done today sits at the bottom, struck through, until tomorrow — crossing things off is the point, and it is how you undo a mis-tick.

  • Tick the box on the row to finish one. Tick it again to bring it back.
  • Double-click the title to fix it. A task's title is yours; a ticket's belongs to its tool, which is why only these can be renamed.
  • Nothing else touches them. No sync, and they never disappear because a tool stopped mentioning them.

On the Map, Tasks has Slate as its source, in the same column as your tools — because it has one, and it is this app.

Reminders

Any item can carry a time — a line you wrote, or a ticket somebody else filed. Remind me… beside Done offers in an hour, this evening, tomorrow morning and next week, or a time you pick. The row then shows when it is due.

When the time comes, Slate rings a notification with the item's title, and the item moves to Needs you until you mark it done. Setting a new time is how you snooze one; Clear the reminder takes it off.

Two things worth knowing:

  • Slate has to be running. Nothing on this Mac rings on Slate's behalf while it is closed. A reminder whose time passed while Slate was shut rings once when you next open it, and says what time it was for rather than pretending it is now.
  • It can be turned off, per workspace, as Reminders in Automations. Times you already set stay on their items.

Your AI tools can set one too: remind\_me in Slate's MCP tools writes a line with a time on it, so "remind me to check this after lunch" lands in the same list from inside Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Gemini or OpenCode.

A reminder is one moment. Something that should happen every day or every week is a routine, which is a different thing and already exists.

The pane

  • Sync at the top reads your tools now, and says when the last read finished, or which tool it could not reach. Pressing it while a read is already running reads again, fully.
  • Map opens the picture of your day.
  • Needs you appears when something is waiting on you right now — a review someone asked you for, or a direct message.
  • Solve, Review and Reply, each with its count, and under each one the tools that feed it. A tool that needs you to sign in says Sign in; one Slate cannot reach says Unavailable, with the reason; one in a gateway you have not turned on says Connect. A section's count only includes tools that answered.

Sign in opens the tool's sign-in page in your browser. While you finish there, the button reads Waiting… and Work checks every 4 seconds. It then says Signed in to Jira, or, after 3 minutes with no answer, asks you to try again. For GitHub it tells you to run gh auth login instead. A sign-in link that Find my work tools brought back opens directly, with no waiting.

The Map

The Map shows the same day as a picture, reading left to right: your tools, the section each one feeds, You, and what is handling your day.

  • Your tools are on the left, each as its own logo with its name beside it, and Slate among them feeding Tasks. A number on a logo is how much is waiting from it. A tool Slate cannot read right now says so under its name — Sign in, Unavailable or Soon — and counts nothing, the same as the list.
  • The four sections are coloured dots — Tasks, Solve, Review, Reply, in the list's own order — with their count above them, or "nothing waiting".
  • You are the disc in the middle, with how much needs you.
  • What is handling your day is on the right: the agents running now, and Memory. Past two agents the second becomes a count, and the Sessions tab lists them all.

Every line is the same thin grey: a line only says what feeds what, and the numbers say how much. Click any logo, dot or name to open its list.

An item

Click a row to open the item beside the list:

  • where it came from — "GitHub assigned this to you", with its comments count and repository;
  • Slate remembers — up to three things from your memory that mention the item's key (ECL-18747). A key is the one string that turns up in a memory only when the memory is about that thing; matching on the title instead ranked ordinary words and returned atoms about something else. A task has no key, so it shows no card — Slate has no memory of something that has never existed anywhere but this list;
  • Open in GitHub opens it in your browser (it is hidden when the tool gave no link); Remind me… puts a time on it; Done takes it off the list here. It comes back if the tool reports a newer change — for an email, a new message in the thread.
  • The card at the bottom is where work starts. Run with AI… opens it. One chip decides what happens — Fix, Implement, Investigate on something to solve, Review on a pull request, Draft a reply on a message, or Just open a session — and writes the words into the box, which you can edit. Those words go to the task as your own note, or, for a session, are typed into the tool on one line and not sent until you send them.
  • Where it runs is filled in for you: the item's own repository, else the place you used last, else your only project. Change it in the same line, and Slate remembers that choice for the next item.
  • The button says which of the two it will do: Start task or Open a session.

The item's description and comments are read in the tool when you open it there. Work does not show a terminal or changes; those belong to the item's session in Sessions.

Tasks (beta)

A task carries an item through a fixed process in a session you can see. Tasks (beta) is on in Automations; turning it off leaves the card offering only Just open a session. Only your click starts a task.

The chips are the kinds of work this repository does. A repository can name its own, in .slate/processes/<name>.md: what it is called, which kind of work it is, and which of its skills runs each step.

---
name: Fix a defect
description: Root cause, the whole class, a receipt
shape: fix
steps:
  plan: research
  build: ship
  check: validation
---

Picking one shows what it will do before it does any of it — every step in order, with the skill beside it and Slate's two approvals among them. Slate owns the order and the approvals; the file says which skill runs each step. A file cannot add, remove or reorder a step, and it cannot replace an approval.

Where a repository writes no process, the chips are the methods it has. Slate reads the skills the repository keeps in .agents/skills/ — the tree every AI tool reads — and offers them by the project's own names, so a repo with a designer-ui-fix skill shows that instead of "Fix". A skill says which kind of work it is with one frontmatter line, slate-task: fix; without it Slate matches on the name and what the skill says it is for. A project with no skills sees Slate's own names, and nothing changes for it.

The agent opens the skill itself, so its words are never copied into a prompt and never go stale. The steps, the two approvals and the Task rules stay Slate's: a project supplies the method, never the process.

The chips, and what the task can use, are shown together:

  • On an item to solve: Fix, Implement or Investigate. An investigation only reports the cause and the options; it changes no file.
  • On a pull request to review: Review. You approve the comments as one of three — Post comments, Request changes or Approve — and the task posts exactly that. Slate never picks for you. The request's own head is fetched from your repository's origin — a fork's branch is never added as a remote — and checked out in a folder of its own. The agent drafts review comments; a review never pushes.
  • On a message to answer: Draft a reply, in a folder of its own under Slate's data, or Find the answer, then draft, which reads the project you pick before it writes. Either way the dialog says up front whether Slate can send it or will hand it to you.
  • Check — the command the check step runs. Slate reads it from the project's files (package.json, Makefile, pyproject.toml, Cargo.toml, go.mod). Change it there and it is saved for the project.
  • The tracker and the code host, and whether your AI tool reaches each one — through its command line (gh, glab), an MCP server, or a claude.ai connector. A tool it cannot reach never blocks the task: the row says what happens instead, such as a link to open the pull request or a ticket update to copy, and Connect takes you to Connect → Sources.

When you click Start task, Slate:

  1. makes a branch named after the item — fix/proj-12-short-title — and a folder of its own for it, then runs the project's setup command there in the background;
  2. starts your AI tool in that folder with one line that names the task. Codex and Cursor are told to trust that folder, so they do not stop to ask;
  3. shows the task in the item: its steps, what the agent last reported, and its status. The item also moves to Tasks, at the top of the list, with the same status.

The first step, the brief, hands the agent up to five things Slate remembers about the item and any earlier task for it.

The agent does one step at a time — Brief, Plan, then Build, Check and Draft — and reports each one. After each step the task waits for Next. After the plan it waits for Approve plan or Send back; the plan's steps and files are shown there, and Save as Plan page keeps it as the branch's Plan page (otherwise the branch page offers it). Before anything leaves your machine the task waits for Approve to ship, which shows exactly what will leave: the branch's commits and changed files, and the pull request title and body, ticket update, review comments or reply, word for word, each with Copy. Approving lets the push to the task's branch through, and the writes to the item's own tracker or chat and the code host; every other server still asks. After shipping, Slate reads the pull request back and says whether it matches what you approved.

Whenever a task stops for you — a step done, a plan or a ship to approve, a held call, a block — it is counted in Needs you in Work and in ⌘⇧J, and your AI tool shows one notification saying which task and what it waits for. (Claude Code sends that notification; the other tools show the task in Needs you only.)

Autopilot runs the steps back to back in the same session, without waiting for Next. It still stops at the two approvals, at a held call, and after a long run of steps, and a failed check goes back to Build up to twice before it stops. Autopilot is off for every project until you turn it on in the start box; then each task can start on it, and the panel switches a running task between Step by step and Autopilot. On Autopilot, Claude Code does not stop to ask for a call the Task rules allow; the rules still hold everything else.

After shipping, a task waits for its merge. Every two minutes Slate reads the pull request's state with gh or glab and the Work item's state; when the request is merged or the item is done, the task is done. Mark done finishes it by hand, and a request closed without a merge blocks the task so you can decide. A finished task writes one line to its branch page's progress log — the pull request and whether it matched what you approved — keeps a lesson if the agent reported one, and records the tokens and estimated cost of its session (Claude Code, Codex and Gemini CLI report usage). A task also shows on its pull request's own item under Review.

Tasks, in Work's side list, shows every task of the last two weeks and counts them: started, done, pull requests and how many matched, the median time to done, the calls the rules held or refused, and the estimated cost. A finished task can give its folder back there with Remove folder; the branch stays.

If you type in the task's session yourself, the task pauses until you press Resume. If the session has ended — you quit Slate, or closed it — Reopen session opens it again in the task's folder: a background Claude Code job that is still running is reattached, otherwise the conversation continues where the tool allows it. A session that never picks the task up within five minutes blocks it, and Resume starts it again.

Next appears only once the agent has finished its turn. A failed check goes back to Build up to twice, then the task stops. A pull request closed without a merge offers Mark done or Watch for the merge again. For a reply or review you posted yourself, I sent it myself finishes the task. Cancel task stops the session before it removes the folder.

The agent keeps everything your tool can normally do — shell, web, your MCP servers — inside Task rules:

  • Git: it works only on the task's branch. A push is refused until you approve shipping.
  • MCP: a tool that writes somewhere else asks you first.
  • Shell: a command that sends data out asks you first.
  • Files: writes stay inside the task's folder.

The rules read each tool call and each shell command, including the commands wrapped inside env, $( ), scripts and heredocs. They are a guard, not a sandbox: a program they do not know can still reach the network. The push rule has a second lock — a git hook in the task's folder — and after shipping Slate reads the pull request back.

A call the rules hold is refused for now and shows in the task. Answer it there: Allow for this step, Always allow in this project, or Deny, and the agent is told to go on. An allowance names that one call — the MCP tool, or the exact command — and only lets through what a rule would have asked about; it never opens anything a rule refuses. Cancel task stops the task's session and removes its folder; its branch stays.

Task rules in the start box lists the built-in rules and the project's own entries. Add one as Allow, Ask first or Deny, naming the call the way the held-call card does: an MCP tool (mcp__jira__add_comment, or mcp__jira__* for all of a server's tools), shell: and a command (shell:npm publish), or file: and a path. Allow names one exact call and only lets through what a built-in rule would have asked about. Ask first and Deny also cover a command's first words and everything under a folder, and Deny always wins.

Where the items come from

No AI tool is involved in a normal read, and no tokens are spent.

GitHub is read through your own GitHub CLI (gh). When gh is signed in to more than one account (the default config and others such as ~/.config/gh-personal), each workspace reads as the account that owns its repos — or, for an organisation's repos, the one that can see them. A workspace whose repos no account reaches shows no GitHub, and switching a workspace to another account drops the items the old one read.

  • Work reads when you open it, and again when you come back to the window after a minute.
  • Each read first asks GitHub whether anything changed since the last one. When nothing did, that check is all it costs. A full read happens when something changed, when the last one is more than ten minutes old, or when you press Sync.
  • Four lists are read: reviews asked of you, pull requests assigned to you, your own open pull requests, and issues assigned to you. A pull request that is on more than one list shows once.
  • If gh is not installed, GitHub shows Unavailable. If it is signed out, it shows Sign in — run gh auth login in a terminal.

Jira, Slack and Gmail are read through the MCP servers your AI tools already use, after you press Connect once. Slate looks in the settings of Claude Code, Cursor, Gemini CLI, Codex and OpenCode — only for the accounts this workspace runs under (its AI tools in Connect, and its projects' own), never another workspace's — and keeps two kinds of server:

  • A tool gateway, which carries many tools. The same gateway declared in several tools, or both as a local server and as a web address, counts once.
  • A server for one work tool, such as a linear or atlassian server. It is skipped when a gateway already reads that tool, or when Slate reads it itself (GitHub, through gh).

Either can be one of two kinds:

  • A local server. Slate starts it itself and keeps it running. Turning Work tools off stops it, unless another workspace still has them on.
  • A web address (streamable HTTP or the older SSE). If its settings carry a key, Slate uses that key. If not, Slate signs in as itself: Sign in opens the server's own sign-in page in your browser, which sends you back to Slate. Slate keeps that sign-in on this machine, one per workspace, and never reads your AI tool's.

Slate only calls tools that read; see the rule.

If the gateway cannot be reached — for example its sign-in expired — its tools say Unavailable or Sign in, and their last items stay until the next good read. Items from a tool that no gateway of this workspace reads any more are deleted from the workspace; while Work tools are off, gateway items leave the list and come back with the next good read.

  • Jira: issues assigned to you that are not done, grouped by Jira's own status names (for example Ready for QA, In Review).
  • Slack: direct messages and mentions from the last 7 days that you have not answered.
  • Gmail: unread emails from the last 7 days, up to 30, by subject and sender. Slate opens at most 15 new emails per read; the rest appear on a later one. Outlook is not read this way.

Reply shows only what belongs to this workspace. A message stays when it names something the workspace works on — a project or repo in your tree (thunderbolt, acme/api), or a Jira project key or name from Solve.

  • Gmail applies this in its own search, to the subject only.
  • A Slack message is checked as it is read, on its text; a link or the channel's name alone does not count, and the text is not kept. A Slack message also stays when it comes from someone in your GitHub items.
  • Reply items read for you by Claude are checked the same way.

A workspace with nothing to match — no projects, and no Solve or Review items — shows everything.

Tools Slate learns on its own

During a sync, a tracker in your gateway that Slate has no reader for gets one try a day in each workspace: if one of its tools says by name that it lists your items (for example get_my_issues), Slate calls it, finds the list, the title and the status in the answer, and keeps that read when it returns at least one item. From then on the tool is read like Jira, for free. Finished items (Done, Closed, Resolved) are left out.

A gateway tool that read fine in the last 3 minutes is not asked again until you press Sync; one waiting on sign-in is always asked. Gmail opens only emails it has not seen.

Find my work tools

Find my work tools shows only while a tool in your gateway is still unread. It asks Claude (Sonnet), once, which tool reads each one and how. It takes a few minutes. Slate then tries each answer itself and keeps only the ones that returned a list; from then on it reads those tools on its own, with no AI tool and no tokens.

Slate checks every tool itself before it calls one — when it picks a tool, when it keeps an answer, and on every later read. A tool whose name sends, creates, updates, deletes, posts, replies, comments, merges, closes, assigns, moves, sets, archives, marks or uploads is refused, and so is a tool that belongs to a different tool than the one being read. Claude is kept away from the same tools while it looks.

A tool only your Claude account reaches (a claude.ai connector, such as monday.com) cannot be read by Slate directly. Slate asks Claude (Haiku) to read those when you open Work and the last read is over 30 minutes old, or when you press Sync (at most every 2 minutes). It waits until Slate has finished listing your gateway's tools, and never reads a tool Slate reads itself. There is no timer in the background.

What is stored

For each item Slate keeps one line: the tool, its key (acme/api#412), the title, the state, the person, the link, a few counts, and the reminder's time if it has one. It never keeps an issue's description, a comment, or message text. A line you wrote yourself is the same row with no tool behind it, and the title you typed is all of it. Items live in the workspace's local file and are not shared, even in a shared workspace.

Today's meetings are never written to disk. Slate holds the title, the times, the link and your own answer in memory until the day ends or Slate quits. It never keeps a meeting's description, notes or guest list.

For each tool learned through Find my work tools, Slate also keeps how to read it: the tool's name, its arguments, and where each field sits in the answer. It never keeps the answer.

For a gateway at a web address, Slate keeps its own sign-in (the token the gateway issued to Slate) in a private file on this machine, work-gateway-auth.json, readable only by your user account. It is never in a workspace file and never shared.

A sign-in you have started but not finished is kept the same way, in oauth-pending.json, so closing Slate or letting an update install itself while the browser tab is open does not lose it. Slate also holds one fixed address for the browser to come back to, so a sign-in still lands after a restart; if another program holds that address, the sign-in comes back to the app's own port instead, as before. Anything you leave unfinished is dropped after fifteen minutes.
