Activity & Analytics

Slate captures your AI sessions as you work. The Activity page is where that history becomes something you can read: what you did today, which projects you touched, and how your work is trending. Everything here is derived from data already on your machine.

The dashboard

Activity is a board of widgets. These are on it out of the box:

WidgetWhat it tells you
Sessions over timeThe trend, over the range you picked
RecordsYour busiest day, best week, longest focus and favourite tool
TimelineRecent sessions and facts, newest first
Last 13 weeksA calendar grid of activity density
Time of day · WeekdayWhen during the day and the week you work
By tool, By project, By branchSessions ranked by the tool that ran them and the project and branch they ran in

Eight more are one click away under Edit: Sessions (the full list), Momentum, By model, Memory facts, Quick stats, and three readings of the memory layer — Memory contract, Recall quality and Corpus health.

Edit, on the page, reorders widgets and hides the ones you don't want. The side pane holds what the board is about: the project picker (All projects, or one project), the range (7d, 30d, 90d, 1y, All) and saved views, where the current range and filter can be kept under a name. Drag across the trend chart to zoom into a narrower window. Clicking a row in any breakdown filters the whole board to it.

Where the numbers come from

From each AI tool's own files, read by Slate's watcher: Claude Code's, Codex's, Gemini's and Cursor's session transcripts, and OpenCode's database. A session is one conversation the tool gave an id, counted once however many files or branches it touched. Days are your local calendar days.

Slate keeps what it reads in machine.db on this computer, and keeps it after the tool deletes its own files — Claude Code removes transcripts after 30 days — so the history here outlasts the tool's. Nothing from it is ever shared, even from a shared workspace.

Slate's in-app agent and the activity tool over the MCP server read the same sessions, so all three agree.

History from before Slate kept it

Codex and OpenCode keep their sessions for good, so their history is exact from the first session they recorded. Claude Code deletes transcripts after 30 days, but keeps every prompt you typed in its prompt history. For a Claude Code session whose transcript is already gone, Slate reads that history: the session, its day, its folder and its prompts — within about 8% of the transcript count, measured where both exist — and nothing about tokens, cost or active time, which the history does not hold.

So a range reaching back before an account's first transcript shows its sessions and active days, and the session-time and token tiles say where those are recorded from ("recorded from 2026-09-09"). A session known only from the prompt history shows "not recorded" for its tokens, and a comparison of session time between periods needs exact records for both.

yarn usage:verify recounts every AI tool file still on disk, independently of the reader that filled the history, and checks the history holds the same numbers per account.
