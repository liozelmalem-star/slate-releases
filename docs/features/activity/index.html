Features

Activity & Analytics

Activity shows your AI sessions as you work: what you did today, which projects you touched, how your work is trending. It is built from data already on your machine.

The dashboard

The board opens on the range you picked in the side panel. One sentence sums up those days: "You ran 12 sessions over 6h, mostly in slate (8 of 12). Busiest at 14:00." "Mostly in" shows only when one project has half the sessions or more. A second line names the one comparison worth a look, such as "Look at: more sessions than the 30 days before (21), and 1h 5m more time in them."

Four cards compare the range with the same number of days before it: sessions, session time, memories learned and active days. On 30d, "−9 vs the 30 days before" compares the last 30 days with the 30 before. All has no days before, so it shows no change. A card also shows no change when the history does not reach that far back (see below).

Below the cards is a board of widgets. These are on it at first:

WidgetWhat it tells you
TimelineThe last 7 days, newest first: sessions, memories learned, finished agent runs, automation reports and artifacts. Empty days are left out. Today and yesterday are open; older days are one line each. An open day lists up to 6 sessions and 4 memories, then "+N more". A session sits under the day it was last active. Click a day in Last 13 weeks to see that day instead, grouped by the day each session started, as the heatmap counts

Show records, under the timeline, adds Records: your busiest day, best week (the best 7 days in the last year), longest focus (your longest single session), favourite tool and top model. Hide records takes them away again.

Momentum starts off the board. It lists up to 6 projects with their sessions in the range and the change against the days before, such as "+3". It shows nothing on All. Its rows are not clickable.

Your widgets, cards you described in Apps → Widgets for Activity, join the board once built. A widget sits at the top of the side column and shows the range and project picked in the side panel. Its ⋯ menu opens it in Apps, or takes it off the board. A widget that has not drawn within 10 seconds says so, with a link to Apps.

The + in the side panel's title turns on editing. A dashed spot at the top of each column lists the cards that can go there; click one to add it. These are your widgets for Activity, cards you took off, and the built-in cards not on the board: Sessions (the full list), Momentum, By model, Memories, Quick stats, and four readings of the memory layer: Do sessions save memory?, Is memory used?, Does memory help? and Are memories reachable?.

While editing you reorder and hide widgets. Reset layout restores the first board. Done ends editing.

The side panel holds the project picker (All projects, or one), the range (7d, 30d, 90d, 1y, All) and saved views: the current range, filter and project kept under a name. The view on screen is marked. Remove view offers Undo. Drag across the trend chart to zoom. Click a row in any breakdown to filter the whole board to it.

Where the numbers come from

Slate's watcher reads each agent's own files: the session transcripts of Claude Code, Codex, Gemini, Cursor and the other tools that write them, and the databases of the tools that keep sessions in SQLite (OpenCode, Kiro, Hermes Agent, Goose and Kilo Code). A session is one conversation the tool gave an id, counted once however many files or branches it touched. It counts when you typed into it and the model answered. Runs nobody typed into (claude -p, codex exec), Slate's own runs and subagent files are not sessions. Days are your local calendar days. Session time is the time between your turns, leaving out any pause over 15 minutes.

Slate keeps what it reads in machine.db on this computer, even after the tool removes its files (Claude Code removes transcripts after 30 days). Nothing from it is ever shared, even from a shared workspace. Slate's in-app agent and the activity tool over the MCP server read the same sessions.

History from before Slate kept it

Codex and OpenCode keep their sessions for good, so their history is exact from the first session. Claude Code removes transcripts after 30 days but keeps every prompt you typed in its prompt history. For a session whose transcript is gone, Slate reads that history: the session, its day, folder and prompts. The count was within 8% of the transcript count where both existed (measured 2026-10-16). Tokens, cost and active time are not in it.

So a range that reaches back before an account's first transcript still shows its sessions and active days. A session known only from prompt history shows "not recorded" for tokens. The session-time card compares with the days before only when both stretches have exact records. The other cards compare as long as any record reaches back that far.

yarn usage:verify recounts every agent file still on disk, independently of the reader that filled the history, and checks that the history holds the same numbers per account.
