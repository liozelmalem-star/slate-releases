Slate is a local-first desktop application. It is built so that your workspace lives on your machine and nothing leaves it without an explicit action on your part. This policy explains what Slate stores, where it stores it, and the narrow set of cases where data crosses the boundary of your device.

The short version: no account is required, your workspace is a file you own, and the only content that ever reaches a server is the pages you specifically choose to share with a shared workspace.

What Slate Stores, and Where

Everything you create in Slate — pages, the block-editor content, missions, captured AI sessions, decisions, facts, agents, workflows, and settings — is stored locally in a single SQLite database file on your device.

Slate stores no embeddings and downloads no machine-learning model. Memory is retrieved by keyword matching computed on your device.

ItemLocation
Workspace databaseSee per-platform paths below
Team sign-in session, if you use oneA permission-restricted file beside the database, never inside it

The workspace database lives at:

macOS    ~/Library/Application Support/app.slate.workspace/workspace.db
Linux    $XDG_DATA_HOME/app.slate.workspace/
Windows  %APPDATA%\app.slate.workspace\

You can override the location with the SLATE_DB_PATH environment variable. The database uses WAL mode, and Slate takes an automatic backup before running schema migrations.

Slate holds no provider API keys. Where Slate drives a command-line tool (see below), that tool holds its own authentication under its own configuration. If you sign in to share with a workspace, the resulting session token is written to a permission-restricted file next to the database rather than inside it, so exporting your workspace cannot hand anyone a live credential.

What Leaves Your Device, and When

Slate's default posture is that nothing leaves the machine. There are exactly four exceptions, and each requires either your action or your opt-in.

  1. Model calls made by your own installed CLIs. Slate's model providers (called "brains") drive command-line tools you have already installed and authenticated — the Claude Code CLI and the Gemini CLI. When you run the in-app AI Console or an agent, the relevant CLI sends your prompt and context to that provider to generate a response. Slate does not hold a raw provider API key; the CLI makes the request under its own account and its own terms. What is sent, and to whom, is governed by the provider you chose (see "Third-Party Model Providers" below).
  1. The contents of a shared workspace you create or join. Slate includes an optional team layer. It does nothing unless you sign in, and even then it applies only to a workspace is unshared until you choose to share it, and an unshared workspace has no counterpart in our database at all — nothing about it is transmitted, and nothing can be. Your Personal workspace begins unshared. Because it is also where automatic capture places work you did not explicitly file there, choosing to share it shows you a count of what would be published before you confirm.

Within a shared workspace, the rows you author are copied to a hosted Postgres database (Supabase) where that workspace's members can read them. What is sent is:

  • Pages — title, block content, a plain-text rendering of that content for search, the document type, and the parent link.
  • Memory atoms — the distilled sentence, its kind, its subject, its confidence, and the title of the page it was learned against.
  • Development activity — commits (subject line, message body, and aggregate counts of files changed and lines added or removed), branch created / deleted / merged / switched events, and AI session records (the tool used, a session identifier, and a one-line summary).
  • Your identity — the display name, email address, and avatar supplied by your sign-in provider.

What is never sent, from any workspace: your page metadata (the properties bag, which is where a project's folder path is held), the absolute path recorded against a commit (the normalised git remote URL is sent in its place), per-file lists of the files you touched or staged, the contents of any AI session transcript, the reasoning text behind a memory atom, and your application settings. The set of activity types that may be stored is enforced by a constraint in the database itself, not only by the application.

What we cannot filter is the text itself. A page's body and a memory atom's sentence are sent as written. If you typed a filesystem path into a page, or an AI tool recorded one inside a decision it wrote, that text is transmitted with the rest of the row — Slate does not inspect or redact prose. The structured places a path is held are withheld; a path written in a sentence is content, and content in a shared workspace is what a shared workspace is for.

Sharing is one-way. Other members can read what you wrote but cannot modify it, and nothing another member writes is ever copied over a page of yours. Moving a page out of a shared workspace withdraws it on the next synchronisation, and Erase my work removes every row you authored in that workspace for every member — though, as with any publication, withdrawal is not retroactive. Access is enforced by database-level row security, so a person who is not a member of a workspace cannot read its contents.

  1. A one-time license activation check (roadmap). A planned licensing flow will validate a license key once, on activation, and store a signed receipt in your OS keychain. After that first check it is designed to work offline. This feature is not shipped yet; when it ships it will contact only the licensing endpoint and will not transmit your workspace content.
  1. Opt-in telemetry. Slate can report anonymous usage counts and crash reports. Both are off until you turn them on, in Settings → About, and they are separate switches. What is sent is a closed list, enforced in code rather than promised here:
  • Usage counts — three events only: the app was opened, a session was captured, a stored memory was used. Each carries a random installation id, the app version, the operating system name, the time, and which AI tool a session used. Nothing else.
  • Crash reports — the type of error (for example TypeError) and where in Slate's own code it happened. The error's own text is never sent: it is written wherever the failure occurred, so it can quote whatever Slate was working on at the time, and no rule about future code could keep that safe. Only the type and the call stack travel, and the stack is reduced to file names and line numbers — directories are stripped, and the line that would have carried the message is dropped.

The installation id is 16 random bytes generated on your machine. It is not derived from your hardware, your hostname, your account, or anything else about you, and you can replace it at any time with the "New id" button beside it.

No page content, no session content, no conversation content, and no file paths are ever included in either channel.

Aside from these four cases, Slate makes no outbound network requests with your workspace data.

No Tracking, No Sale of Data

Slate requires no account to use. An account exists only if you choose to share with a team, and no backend receives your pages, sessions, or files unless you have marked them for a workspace.

  • There is no behavioral analytics, ad tracking, profiling, or background monitoring.
  • Your content is never used to train shared, public, or third-party AI models by Slate.
  • Your data is never sold, rented, or traded.

The filesystem watcher that captures AI sessions (from Claude, Codex, Gemini or Cursor conversation files) runs locally on your machine and writes only into your local workspace database.

Third-Party Model Providers

When you use a brain, the underlying CLI communicates with that provider's service. Those interactions are governed by the provider's own terms and privacy policy, not by this document:

  • The Claude Code CLI communicates with Anthropic under Anthropic's terms.
  • The Gemini CLI communicates with Google under Google's terms.

You choose which brain to use, and you authenticate those tools yourself. Similarly, any external MCP server you connect to Slate operates under its own terms; data you send to a connected tool is handled by whoever runs that tool.

If you use the team layer, the pages you share are stored by Supabase, and sign-in is handled by the provider you pick (GitHub or Google). Those services process that data under their own terms and privacy policies.

Your Control, Export, and Deletion

Because your workspace is a single local file, you are in full control of it.

  • Export — Settings → Workspaces → Manage → Storage offers a one-click copy of workspace.db. Because it is just a SQLite file, your data is portable and readable with standard tools.
  • Open the folder — Settings → Workspaces → Manage → Storage can reveal the workspace folder directly.
  • Deletion — deleting the workspace database removes your local data. Uninstalling Slate stops all local processing, including the session watcher and any scheduled agents.
  • Withdrawing shared content — moving a page out of a shared workspace removes it from that workspace on the next synchronisation.
  • Stop sharing — switching sharing off for a workspace stops it publishing anything further and erases what you have already published there. The workspace and its contents remain on your device.
  • Erasure — Erase my work on a shared workspace deletes every page, memory atom and activity record you authored there, for every member. Deleting content locally has the same effect on the copies: the next synchronisation withdraws from our database anything your device no longer holds. It is offered separately from leaving a workspace, which removes only your own machine's copy and deletes nothing upstream.

Unless you have created or joined a shared workspace, there is no server-side copy to delete. If you have, the copies are the rows you authored in it, and they are removed when you erase or withdraw them.
