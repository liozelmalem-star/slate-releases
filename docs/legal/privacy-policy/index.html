This is a template/placeholder legal document for a product still in development. It is not legal advice and does not create a contract. It should be reviewed and completed by qualified counsel before any commercial release, and jurisdiction, contact, and licensing details filled in.

Slate is a local-first desktop application. It is built so that your workspace lives on your machine and nothing leaves it without an explicit action on your part. This policy explains what Slate stores, where it stores it, and the narrow set of cases where data crosses the boundary of your device.

The short version: you sign in once with Google or GitHub, your account holds your name, email and plan, your workspace is a file you own, and the only workspace content that ever reaches a server is what is in a workspace you specifically choose to share.

Your Account#

Slate requires an account. On first launch you sign in with Google or GitHub. Sign-in is handled by Supabase Auth, in Slate's own Supabase project. The account holds:

  • your name, email address and picture link, as supplied by Google or GitHub;
  • which of the two providers you signed in with;
  • your plan (Free, or Team when it is available);
  • if you asked to hear when Team is ready, that you asked and from which screen.

The account holds none of your docs, sessions or memories. The sign-in session is kept in a permission-restricted file (cloud-session.json) in Slate's application-data folder, never inside a database, so later launches work offline.

When you are online, Slate contacts its Supabase project at each start and whenever the sign-in token renews. It renews the token, makes sure your account exists, and reads which workspaces you belong to. Those requests carry your account id and your IP address, and no workspace content. Supabase Auth also keeps sign-in logs that hold your IP address.

Invitations. When you invite someone to a shared workspace, their email address and the role you chose are stored in our database until they accept or decline. Slate sends no email: the invitation opens in your own mail app, and you send it.

What Slate Stores, and Where#

Everything you create in Slate — docs, the block-editor content, agent runs, captured AI sessions, decisions, memories, automations, skills, and settings — is stored locally in SQLite database files on your device: workspace.db for your Personal workspace, one more file for each shared workspace, and machine.db for the settings that describe this computer.

Slate stores no embeddings and downloads no machine-learning model. Memory is retrieved by keyword matching computed on your device, re-ordered by word statistics that Slate fits in memory from your own stored memories and never writes to disk or sends anywhere.

ItemLocation
Workspace databaseSee per-platform paths below
Sign-in sessionA permission-restricted file (cloud-session.json) in Slate's application-data folder, never inside a database

The workspace database lives at:

macOS    ~/Library/Application Support/app.slate.workspace/workspace.db
Linux    $XDG_DATA_HOME/app.slate.workspace/
Windows  %APPDATA%\app.slate.workspace\

You can override the location with the SLATE_DB_PATH environment variable. The database uses WAL mode, and Slate takes an automatic backup before running schema migrations.

Slate holds no provider API keys. Where Slate drives a command-line tool (see below), that tool holds its own authentication under its own configuration. Your Slate sign-in session token is written to a permission-restricted file in Slate's application-data folder rather than inside any database, so exporting your workspace cannot hand anyone a live credential.

What Leaves Your Device, and When#

Slate's default posture is that nothing leaves the machine. Apart from your sign-in (above), four standing exceptions run without a click each time. Three of them need your choice or your opt-in first. The update check runs by default, and you can switch it off. A problem report you send, requests you start yourself and connections you set up are described after the four.

  1. Model calls made by your own installed CLIs. Slate's model providers (called "agents") drive command-line tools you have already installed and authenticated — the agents you connect (16 supported, see the docs). When you use Home, a session, an agent run or an automation, the relevant CLI sends your prompt and context to that provider to generate a response, when you ask it to or on a schedule you set. A cloud session sends your project's code to the vendor's computer (Claude Code clones the project's GitHub remote, or uploads the code if there is none). On my phone connects a session to claude.ai, and Anthropic keeps that conversation. Slate does not hold a raw provider API key; the CLI makes the request under its own account and its own terms. What is sent, and to whom, is governed by the provider you chose (see "Third-Party Model Providers" below).
  1. The contents of a shared workspace you create or join. Slate includes an optional team layer. Sharing a workspace needs the Team plan. Every workspace is unshared until you choose to share it, and a workspace that has never been shared has no counterpart in our database at all — nothing about it is transmitted, and nothing can be. Your Personal workspace begins unshared. Choosing to share it shows you a count of what would be published before you confirm.

Within a shared workspace, the rows you author are copied to a hosted Postgres database (Supabase) where that workspace's members can read them. What is sent is:

  • Docs — title, block content, a plain-text rendering of that content for search, the document type, and the parent link.
  • Memories — the saved sentence, its kind, its subject, its confidence, for a lesson the trigger that says when it applies, and the title of the doc it was learned against.
  • Development activity — commits (the subject line Slate recorded, and any further lines of the message it holds), branch created / deleted / merged / switched events, and a record that an AI session happened (its branch and the time).
  • Artifacts — the docs an agent publishes to be looked at: the title, the kind (HTML or Markdown), the description, the name of the agent that made it, the doc it was made under, and the content of its newest version.
  • Your identity — the display name, email address, and avatar supplied by your sign-in provider.

What is never sent, from any workspace: your doc metadata (the properties bag, which is where a project's or a group's folder path is held), the absolute path recorded against a commit (the normalised git remote URL is sent in its place), counts of files and lines changed, per-file lists of the files you touched or staged, the contents of any AI session transcript and the doc a captured AI session is stored as, the reasoning text behind a memory, any memory that belongs to no doc, any memory you hid from sessions, any memory an app saved, and your application settings. The set of activity types that may be stored is enforced by a constraint in the database itself, not only by the application.

What we cannot filter is the text itself. A doc's body and a memory's sentence are sent as written. If you typed a filesystem path into a doc, or an agent recorded one inside a decision it wrote, that text is transmitted with the rest of the row — Slate does not inspect or redact prose. The structured places a path is held are withheld; a path written in a sentence is content, and content in a shared workspace is what a shared workspace is for.

Sharing is one-way. Other members can read what you wrote but cannot modify it, and nothing another member writes is ever copied over a doc of yours. Moving a doc out of a shared workspace withdraws it on the next synchronisation, and Erase my work removes every row you authored in that workspace for every member — though, as with any publication, withdrawal is not retroactive. Access is enforced by database-level row security, so a person who is not a member of a workspace cannot read its contents.

  1. The update check. Slate asks its update service, and the release host if the service does not answer, whether a newer version exists. No workspace content is sent. The request carries your app version, your operating system and processor type, whether you run a prerelease, and a random install id. The install id is 16 random bytes generated on your machine the first time Slate starts, kept in the file install-id in Slate's data folder. It is not derived from your hardware, your hostname, your account or anything else about you, and it is not the telemetry id below. Its only use is to place your copy in a stable group when a new version is offered to part of the installed copies first. The update service stores no record of it, but the id is part of the request, so it appears, with your IP address, in the request logs that the service's host (Supabase) keeps for a short time. Deleting the file makes Slate generate a new id. Before it installs a downloaded update, Slate asks the service whether that version was withdrawn, sending only the version number. Like any web request, these reveal your IP address and that you run Slate. It runs eight seconds after the window opens and every four hours while it stays open, and whenever you press "Check now" in Settings → About. When a newer version exists, Slate also downloads it from the release host, GitHub Releases, in the background, and installs it when you next quit. You can switch the automatic check and download off in Automations › Built-in (Automatic updates); the manual check still works.
  1. Opt-in telemetry. Slate can report anonymous usage counts and crash reports. Both are off until you turn them on, in Settings → About, and they are separate switches. What is sent is a closed list, enforced in code rather than promised here:
  • Usage counts — three events only: Slate was opened, a session was captured, a memory was recalled. Each carries a random installation id, the app version, the operating system name, the time, and, for a captured session or a recall, which AI tool it was: one of the 16 agents Slate supports, or other. Nothing else.
  • Crash reports — the type of error (for example TypeError) and where in Slate's own code it happened. The error's own text is never sent: it is written wherever the failure occurred, so it can quote whatever Slate was working on at the time, and no rule about future code could keep that safe. Only the type and the call stack travel, and the stack is reduced to its call lines, at most twenty. Each keeps its function name, file name and line number. Directories are stripped, and every line that is not a call line is dropped, the one that would have carried the message included.

The installation id is 16 random bytes generated on your machine. It is not derived from your hardware, your hostname, your account, or anything else about you, and you can replace it at any time with the "New id" button beside it. To have counts already sent deleted, email that id to the contact below before you press "New id"; otherwise they are deleted after 180 days.

The id and both consent switches are per machine, not per workspace — they live in machine.db, the same file that holds your other app-level settings, never inside workspace.db. Deleting a workspace database does not touch them: turning both switches off, or pressing "New id", is how you reset telemetry. Deleting machine.db itself removes the id and both switches along with every other machine-level setting.

No doc content, no session content, no conversation content, and no file paths are ever included in either channel.

A problem report you send. When you press Send report in the report dialog, Slate sends what you wrote, which screen you were on (which can name a project and a branch), the text of the error you reported from (if any), your Slate version, your macOS version and your chip, and, unless you untick it, the last 40 lines of the app log. Every file path in the error and the log lines is replaced by ~/… before the dialog shows them. That is the only cleaning: the log lines and the error text are otherwise sent as written. The dialog shows every line that is sent before you send it, and you can untick the log. A report carries no doc, session or conversation content, no account details and no installation id. It is sent only when you press Send, whether or not telemetry is on, and is stored in Slate's own Supabase project, readable only by the Slate team. What you wrote, the screen, the error and the version are then copied to a private GitHub issue, so the team can track the fix; the log lines stay in Supabase. When you turned crash reports on, a crash's type and stack may be copied to a private GitHub issue in the same way.

Requests you start yourself. When you press a button for it, git and gh fetch, pull, push and open pull requests against your own remote. A widget's GitHub action (approve, request changes, comment or label) runs only after you confirm the card that names the account. A live artifact can load a script from cdnjs, jsDelivr or unpkg, and that host sees the address it asks for.

Connections you set up yourself. Tasks reads your integrations, and Slate makes some of those requests itself. The first two kinds start when you connect the tool, and Disconnect stops them. For an app you connected with a pasted token, Slate sends read requests with that token to the address you gave it, and follows no link that leaves that address. For a tool gateway at a web address, and for an app you connected from Slate, it signs in with the token that server issued to Slate and sends it only to that server. GitHub is read through your own gh sign-in. A toolkit entry already in an agent's own MCP configuration is asked for its list of tools at its own address, with the key that configuration already holds, when Tasks syncs and when you let a widget use one of its tools. What these requests carry is what the other server needs to answer. None of them sends a doc, a session or a memory of yours.

Aside from your sign-in, the four exceptions, a problem report you send, the requests you start and the connections described above, Slate makes no outbound network requests with your workspace data.

No Tracking, No Sale of Data#

Slate requires an account (see Your Account). The account holds who you are and your plan, and no backend receives your docs, sessions, or files unless you have shared the workspace they are in.

  • There is no behavioral analytics, ad tracking, profiling, or background monitoring.
  • Your content is never used to train shared, public, or third-party AI models by Slate.
  • Your data is never sold, rented, or traded.

The filesystem watcher that captures AI sessions (from the conversation files of the AI tools you connect) runs locally on your machine and writes only into your local workspace database.

Third-Party Model Providers#

When you use an agent, the underlying CLI communicates with that provider's service. Those interactions are governed by the provider's own terms and privacy policy, not by this document:

  • The Claude Code CLI communicates with Anthropic under Anthropic's terms.
  • The Codex CLI communicates with OpenAI under OpenAI's terms.
  • The Cursor CLI communicates with Cursor under Cursor's terms.
  • The Gemini CLI communicates with Google under Google's terms.
  • The OpenCode CLI communicates with whichever provider you configured in it, under that provider's terms.
  • The GitHub Copilot CLI communicates with GitHub under GitHub's terms.
  • Every other agent you connect communicates with its own vendor, or the provider you configured in it, under that party's terms.

You choose which agent to use, and you authenticate those tools yourself. Similarly, any external MCP server you connect to Slate operates under its own terms; data you send to a connected tool is handled by whoever runs that tool.

Your Control, Export, and Deletion#

Because each workspace is a single local file, you are in full control of it.

  • Export — Settings → Workspaces, then the workspace, then Storage offers a one-click copy of that workspace's file (workspace.db for Personal). Because it is just a SQLite file, your data is portable and readable with standard tools.
  • Open the folder — the same Storage tab can open Slate's data folder directly.
  • Deletion — deleting a workspace's database file removes that workspace's local data. The uninstall command keeps your workspace files unless you pass --delete-workspace; with it, the folder ~/Library/Application Support/app.slate.workspace/ goes, with machine.db, the shared workspaces' files and the sign-in files in it. A Personal workspace you moved elsewhere is not in that folder and stays. Uninstalling Slate stops all local processing, including the session watcher and any scheduled automations. Without that flag it does not touch the telemetry id or consent switches described above — those live in machine.db, a separate per-machine file, and are reset the way that section describes, not by deleting a workspace.
  • Withdrawing shared content — moving a doc out of a shared workspace removes it from that workspace on the next synchronisation.
  • Stop sharing — switching sharing off for a workspace stops it publishing anything further and withdraws what you have already published there: the rows in our database are blanked, and every member's copy drops them on their next synchronisation. The workspace and its contents remain on your device. If you switch sharing on again, the next synchronisation publishes them again.
  • Erasure — Erase my work on a shared workspace deletes every doc, memory and activity record you authored there, for every member. Deleting content locally has the same effect on the copies: the next synchronisation withdraws from our database anything your device no longer holds. It is offered separately from leaving a workspace, which removes only your own machine's copy and deletes nothing upstream.
  • Account deletion — Settings → Account → Delete my account forever removes your Slate account from our database: your sign-in identity, name, email, plan and Team interest, your memberships, the rows you authored in shared workspaces, invitations you sent or that are waiting for you, any account only you were in, and the sign-in audit records that hold your email and IP address. It signs you out and leaves everything on your device. While you own an account that has other members, Slate asks you to transfer it or remove them first. Some copies sit outside our database and are not reached by it: request logs kept by our hosting providers, copies already on teammates' devices (which drop withdrawn rows on their next synchronisation), anonymous usage counts keyed to this installation (deleted after 180 days), and problem reports you chose to send, with their issue copies.

Because every person signs in, our database holds a record of your account even if you never share anything: your profile (name, email and picture link), your account and plan, and your Team interest, and Supabase Auth keeps sign-in logs with your IP address. Delete my account forever removes them, along with the shared rows you authored. Your email address cannot be changed in Slate, because it comes from Google or GitHub. Your workspaces' contents are on our servers only if you shared a workspace or joined one, and those copies are the rows you authored, removed or blanked when you erase, withdraw or delete your account.
